\section{Introduction}\label{sec1}

Artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted diagnostic support is increasingly embedded into clinical management systems, including veterinary practice, where automated recommendations directly influence treatment decisions taken by professionals and communicated to non-technical stakeholders such as clinic staff and pet owners. The SGCV-IA system --- a real-world veterinary clinic management system developed for a client organization in the province of Los R\'ios, Ecuador --- incorporates an AI-based diagnostic module whose outputs must be trusted and actionable by users with markedly different technical backgrounds. Studying Requirements Engineering in this context matters because, in AI-assisted clinical settings, the absence of explicit and verifiable explainability requirements can directly undermine user trust and, ultimately, patient (animal) safety.

Despite growing attention to explainability in software engineering, most existing work operationalizes explainability as a general software quality attribute rather than as a set of verifiable, stakeholder-specific non-functional requirements (NFRs) elicited through empirical fieldwork. Chazette et al.~\cite{chazette2021} propose a definition, model, and knowledge catalogue for explainability as a software quality concern, and Chazette et al.~\cite{chazette2022} extend this into an end-to-end framework spanning requirements analysis to system evaluation. However, empirical validation of this framework in real-world, resource-constrained, Latin American clinical domains --- and specifically in veterinary informatics --- remains scarce, leaving a gap in understanding how explainability requirements should be operationalized and validated for heterogeneous user profiles (technical vs.\ non-technical) in such settings.

This work makes the following contributions: (1) an empirical characterization of explainability requirements for a real-world AI-assisted veterinary diagnostic system, differentiated by technical and non-technical user profiles; (2) a set of explainability NFRs operationalized as verifiable requirements and validated across two rounds with real users; and (3) an open, FAIR-compliant replication package supporting further empirical work in explainability requirements engineering in under-studied domains.

Accordingly, this study addresses two research questions: \textbf{RQ1} asks what types of explanations technical and non-technical users of the SGCV-IA system require to trust the automated decisions of the AI diagnostic module; \textbf{RQ2} asks to what extent explainability requirements, operationalized as verifiable NFRs, achieve adequate coverage and perceived satisfaction across the two validation rounds. Preliminary fieldwork suggests that explainability needs diverge meaningfully between user profiles, a finding developed further in Section~\ref{sec4}.

\section{Methodology}\label{sec3}

This study follows the case study guidelines of Runeson and H\"ost~\cite{runeson2009}, as prescribed by the course guide for Approach 3 (explainability) projects, given the single-case, real-world, multi-stakeholder nature of the SGCV-IA system.

\subsection{Research questions (PICOC)}\label{subsec1}

The research questions are formulated using the PICOC framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Context):

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Population (P):} Users of an AI-assisted veterinary clinical management system, stratified into two profiles: technical users (veterinary professionals interacting directly with the AI diagnostic module) and non-technical users (administrative staff and pet owners interacting with system outputs indirectly).
  \item \textbf{Intervention (I):} A set of explainability mechanisms operationalized as verifiable non-functional requirements (NFRs), integrated into the SGCV-IA AI diagnostic module.
  \item \textbf{Comparison (C):} Perceived satisfaction and coverage of explainability dimensions between technical and non-technical user profiles, and between two validation rounds.
  \item \textbf{Outcome (O):} Coverage of the explainability framework's dimensions by verifiable NFRs; Likert-scale satisfaction per dimension; inter-round agreement.
  \item \textbf{Context (C):} A real-world, single-organization veterinary clinic deployment in Ecuador, evaluated within an undergraduate Requirements Engineering course project (ISR-401, UTEQ).
\end{itemize}

\noindent Research questions:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{RQ1.} What types of explanations do technical and non-technical users of the SGCV-IA system require to trust the automated decisions of the AI diagnostic module?
  \item \textbf{RQ2.} To what extent do explainability requirements, operationalized as verifiable NFRs, achieve adequate coverage and perceived satisfaction across the two validation rounds?
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Study design}\label{subsec2}

We adopt a \textbf{single embedded case study} design~\cite{runeson2009}, with the SGCV-IA system as the single case and the two user profiles (technical / non-technical) as embedded units of analysis. The case study unfolds in two validation rounds: (1) an initial round eliciting explainability needs and mapping them to candidate NFRs, and (2) a confirmatory round validating the operationalized NFRs against the explainability framework of Chazette et al.~\cite{chazette2021,chazette2022}. Deviations from the protocol pre-registered in OSF (Entrega 3, 2A) will be reported explicitly in Section~\ref{subsec6}.

\subsection{Participants and recruitment}\label{subsec3}

Participants are recruited from the real client organization associated with the SGCV-IA project (a veterinary clinic in the province of Los Ríos, Ecuador), stratified into two profiles:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Technical users:} veterinary professionals who interact directly with the AI diagnostic module's outputs and rationale.
  \item \textbf{Non-technical users:} administrative/clinic staff and pet owners who interact with system outputs indirectly (e.g., through reports or client-facing summaries).
\end{itemize}

\noindent \textbf{Inclusion criteria:} active involvement with the target organization's clinical or administrative workflow during the study period; informed consent signed in accordance with Ecuador's Ley Orgánica de Protección de Datos Personales~\cite{lopdp2021}. \textbf{Exclusion criteria:} participants under 18 years of age; participants unable to provide informed consent. Minimum sample size targets follow the course guide's terminal empirical minimums (Section 3 of the Entrega 4 guide): $\geq$16 accumulated interview participants across profiles, with at least three well-represented profiles.

\subsection{Instruments}\label{subsec4}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Semi-structured interview guide}, eliciting explainability needs per user profile (full guide in supplementary material).
  \item \textbf{Structured validation questionnaire}, using 5-point Likert-scale items per explainability dimension of the framework in~\cite{chazette2021}, applied in both validation rounds.
  \item \textbf{Member-checking protocol}, confirming interpretation of round-two results with at least three previously interviewed participants~\cite{birt2016}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Data analysis plan}\label{subsec5}

The analysis plan, pre-registered in the OSF protocol (Entrega 3, 2A), is as follows:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Coverage.} The proportion of explainability dimensions of~\cite{chazette2021} covered by at least one verifiable NFR will be computed and reported descriptively.
  \item \textbf{Perceived satisfaction.} Mean Likert score per dimension will be reported with 95\% confidence intervals.
  \item \textbf{Inter-round agreement.} Agreement between the two validation rounds will be quantified with Fleiss' $\kappa$~\cite{fleiss1971}.
  \item \textbf{Technical vs. non-technical comparison.} Differences in perceived satisfaction between technical and non-technical users will be examined with the Mann-Whitney $U$ test, with effect size $r$ derived from the test statistic, following the operationalization of~\cite{chazette2022}.
  \item \textbf{Assumption checks.} Shapiro-Wilk for normality and Levene's test for homogeneity of variances will be reported for all quantitative comparisons, consistent with the common elements required across all three approaches (Section 4.4 of the course guide).
  \item All descriptive statistics (median, mean, standard deviation, min, max, IQR), exact test names and statistics, $p$-values to at least three decimals, effect sizes, and 95\% confidence intervals will be reported per the course guide's reporting requirements.
\end{itemize}

All figures and tables in the Results section will be generated exclusively by versioned analysis scripts in \texttt{06\_Experimento/scripts\_analisis/} (R or Python), reproducing exactly the reported values; no manually produced tables or pasted figures will be included, per the course guide's integrity requirements.
